1. Earth spins on a tilted axis — day and night

Earth rotates once every ~24 hours around an axis tilted 23.5° to its orbital plane. The half facing the Sun has day; the half in shadow has night. As Earth spins, the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west — but really it is Earth that is turning.

2. Earth orbits the Sun — the year and the seasons

Earth travels once around the Sun every 365.25 days. Its axis keeps pointing at the same spot in space (toward Polaris, roughly), so as Earth moves around the orbit, first the northern hemisphere leans toward the Sun (June solstice, summer there) and then away (December solstice). The equinoxes (March/September) are when both hemispheres get equal daylight.

3. The Sun appears to move through the zodiac

Because Earth is moving, the Sun's position against the background stars changes ~1° per day along a circle called the ecliptic. Western (tropical) astrology divides this circle into 12 equal signs starting at the March equinox. Astronomy recognizes the same ecliptic but the actual constellations are unequal in size — and there is a 13th: Ophiuchus, which the Sun passes through about Nov 29 – Dec 17.

4. Why the night sky changes with the seasons

At midnight, you look away from the Sun, out into the part of space opposite the Sun's direction. Since Earth is on the opposite side of its orbit six months later, the midnight sky faces the opposite direction — so winter skies show Orion and Sirius while summer skies show Scorpius and the Milky Way's core. The "leading star" of each season changes accordingly.

5. Precession, pole stars & the 13th constellation

Earth's axis slowly wobbles like a spinning top — one full cycle takes about 25,772 years (axial precession). This slowly shifts where the axis points (so Polaris will not always be the pole star) and shifts the position of the equinoxes against the constellations: the March equinox is currently in Pisces, drifting toward Aquarius — the basis of the "Age of Aquarius" idea.

Illustration only: the celestial pole traces a 47° circle across the millennia.

12-sign horoscope vs 13 constellations. Horoscope signs are fixed to the seasons (tropical): Aries always begins at the March equinox. The actual constellations along the ecliptic have drifted since the zodiac was defined roughly 2,000 years ago, and the Sun also passes through Ophiuchus (the Serpent-bearer, between Scorpius and Sagittarius) — the 13th constellation astronomers note on the ecliptic. Your horoscope sign is a seasonal marker, not the constellation physically behind the Sun on your birthday.
